How it works

Zone order first, stop order second

Every drop-off belongs to a delivery zone such as D-8.3E, and drivers finish one zone before starting the next. The whole problem turns on predicting the order of those zones.

1

Learn from past routes

Three small choice models are fitted on historical routes: which zone a driver goes to next, which zone a route starts in, and which it ends in.

2

Pick the most likely zone order

For a new route, the zone order with the lowest total learned cost is found with a route solver over the zones.

3

Sequence the stops

The same solver builds the shortest stop sequence that serves the zones in that order.

What the model looks at

  • History. How often drivers from the same station went from one zone to the other before, and how often a zone was first or last.
  • Zone codes. Whether two zones share a region, and whether only the sub-area number or only the block letter changes between them.
  • Distance. Travel time between zones and which zone is nearest.

Why zone order is the lever

If the driver's own zone order is given, even a simple stop order inside each zone scores –. The learned model scores – and the shortest route –. Almost all of the remaining gap is zone-order prediction: the model gets the first zone right on – of routes and – of zone-to-zone moves.

How it is tested

One training route in five is held out by a hash of its id. The model is fitted on the other – routes and never sees the held-out ones, neither their sequences nor their zone history. Scores use the challenge's official metric, checked against the official scoring code.

Limits

  • These are held-out training routes, not the challenge's 3,072 official evaluation routes.
  • Time windows and package sizes are not used yet.
